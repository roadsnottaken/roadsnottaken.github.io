Essay · The future (contains labelled speculation)
Part of a nonpartisan civic project for the October 17, 2026 Vancouver election. It doesn't endorse any candidate or party. Passages marked as speculation are the author's opinion, not forecasts.

The City That Kept Its Options Open: Vancouver's Next Fifty Years

Most big North American cities spend their future paying for their past. Seattle spent years and a lot of money replacing the Alaskan Way Viaduct with a tunnel. Boston paid for the Big Dig. Toronto is still arguing over the Gardiner. A great deal of urban ambition on this continent goes into undoing what the expressway era built.

Vancouver, the only major North American city without a freeway running into its core, carries less of that debt. Its waterfront is largely public. Its grid is small-scale and fine-grained, with back lanes. Walking and transit already carry a large share of daily life. None of this guarantees a good future. It does mean the city starts the next fifty years with more room to manoeuvre than almost any of its peers, and the question is what it does with that room.

This essay is about that question. Where I describe policies, projects and timelines, I've tried to stick to what is on the public record as of October 2026. Where I imagine what might come next, I'll say clearly that I'm speculating.

Finishing the Story: The Viaducts and Northeast False Creek

The Georgia and Dunsmuir viaducts, opened in 1971 and 1972, are the only pieces of the old freeway plan ever built. Council voted on October 27, 2015 to remove them, and in 2018 it approved the Northeast False Creek Plan to replace them. That plan calls for an at-grade street network linking downtown to the water, 32 acres of new and renewed parks and open space, about 1,800 units of social housing, and a neighbourhood shaped by reconciliation with local First Nations and cultural redress for the Black and Chinese Canadian communities. Part of that redress is a City memorandum of understanding with the Hogan's Alley Society for the 898 Main Street block. Hogan's Alley, the heart of Vancouver's Black community, was largely erased to build the viaducts. The MOU envisions housing and a cultural centre there.

The honest status report is less inspiring. As of this month the viaducts are still standing, and the City says their removal timeline "is still uncertain." Funding depends heavily on redevelopment by landowners such as Concord Pacific, and a weak condo market and rising construction costs have stalled it. In 2018 the City estimated the street-network changes at $438 million, and staff have said that figure needs to be reassessed. In January 2025 Concord unveiled a revised concept called Concord Landing: about 5,000 homes in 12 towers, some as tall as 65 storeys, around a pedestrian village and a completed stretch of seawall. City officials have since been meeting regularly with Concord and talking with senior governments about financing. Meanwhile crews began replacing expansion joints on the Georgia Viaduct on October 2, with work expected to last until spring 2027. The demolition plan is still alive, but nobody has scheduled it.

Here is where I start speculating. The removal will probably happen eventually, because the land is too valuable and the structures too old to justify keeping them forever. The more interesting question is what kind of place replaces them. One version is simply more towers by the water: successful, expensive and a little generic. A better version would build on what already surrounds the site, which is Chinatown, Strathcona, the stadiums, Science World, the new St. Paul's Hospital going up on the False Creek Flats, and a revived Hogan's Alley block, all connected by ordinary streets instead of ramps. If Vancouver gets this right, the last remnant of its freeway era could become a neighbourhood that openly acknowledges the harm done there and gives some of the land back to the communities it was taken from. Few cities get to tell that story about themselves.

Transit: The Broadway Subway and Beyond

The single biggest near-term change is underground. The Broadway Subway is a 5.7-kilometre extension of the Millennium Line from VCC–Clark to Arbutus Street, with six new underground stations. Its trackwork was finished in September 2026, train testing began in May, and it is scheduled to open for passenger service in fall 2027. The province says it will have three times the capacity of the 99 B-Line it partly replaces, and the 99 has been one of the most crowded bus routes in the region.

The obvious next step is UBC. A roughly seven-kilometre extension from Arbutus to the university, with stations along the route including one planned for the Jericho Lands, is still in planning and design under the provincial Ministry of Transportation and Transit. The federal and provincial governments have funded that planning work, but as of this autumn there is no full construction funding commitment. Across the inlet, TransLink is studying rapid transit to the North Shore as part of a Burrard Inlet Rapid Transit program that could eventually include a dedicated transit crossing. Half a century after Vancouverites killed a freeway tunnel near Brockton Point, the new third-crossing debate is about trains and buses instead of lanes into downtown.

Speculation again: if the UBC line and a North Shore connection both get built in the next twenty years, Vancouver could reach mid-century with something close to a complete rapid-transit grid in its core. You could cross the city east–west and north–south without a car, and most new housing would sit within walking distance of a station. That would make the decision not to build freeways look less like a refusal and more like a plan.

A Connected Region

Vancouver doesn't stand alone. The city's freeway-free core works because the whole region is tied together by transit, and that network is still growing.

The backbone already exists. The Expo Line runs from Waterfront through Burnaby and New Westminster to Surrey. The Millennium Line serves Burnaby and the Tri-Cities as far as Lafarge Lake–Douglas in Coquitlam. The Canada Line connects downtown to Richmond and YVR. The 16-kilometre Surrey–Langley extension, recently named the Dave Barrett Extension, is under construction along Fraser Highway. It will have eight stations and is expected to open in late 2029. Once it opens, Langley City Centre will have a direct SkyTrain connection to downtown Vancouver.

The water is part of the network. The SeaBus has crossed Burrard Inlet since 1977. Its newest vessel, the Burrard Chinook, entered service in 2021 as Canada's first fully hybrid-electric SeaBus, and TransLink is designing a battery-electric next-generation vessel.

From here on, this section is speculation. Imagine electric passenger ferries linking a ring of waterfront nodes: Horseshoe Bay and the North Shore, Port Moody and Deep Cove up the inlet, and New Westminster, Ladner and Steveston on the Fraser. Waterways that now divide the region could become some of its quietest, most reliable routes.

Tunnels for transit, not for cars. This is speculative, and nobody has proposed it. Picture a dense mesh rather than a few big trunk lines: many small-bore tunnels and many surface portals. Robotaxis and autonomous TransLink shuttles would run point to point beneath busy arterials, then come up close to where people actually want to go, whether a school in Newton, a clinic in Kerrisdale or a ferry dock in Ladner. SkyTrain would remain the backbone. The mesh would handle the in-between trips, the ones that now push people into cars.

This wouldn't bring back the freeway by another route. It would be the next step after the freeway fight. The 1967 plan would have pushed private cars through neighbourhoods at the cost of homes and waterfront. A mesh like this would carry shared, electric, automated vehicles under neighbourhoods and leave the surface for people.

The engineering constraints are real. Whether a mesh like this is even feasible is an open question, for at least four reasons:

  • Soft, wet ground. Much of Richmond and Delta sits on Fraser River delta sediments. In a geotechnical memo for the Massey crossing, engineers describe a water table "close to grade" and a top layer of sand, roughly 30 metres thick, that can liquefy in an earthquake. They also note that a deep soft-ground bored tunnel there would be at "the current upper boundaries" of tunnel-boring technology.
  • Earthquakes. Every tunnel in the region has to be designed for seismic shaking. The seismic retrofit of the George Massey Tunnel is a reminder of how much work that takes in delta soils.
  • Water crossings. The routes across Burrard Inlet and the Fraser are the hardest and most expensive kind of tunnelling. Metro Vancouver's Port Mann water supply tunnel under the Fraser ran into groundwater pressure that its engineers called the highest of any soft-ground tunnel in Canada at the time.
  • No precedent at this scale. The Boring Company's only operating system, the Vegas Loop, currently lists 14 stations. It runs under the dry ground of the Las Vegas resort corridor, and a 2026 report put the tunnels in active use at about 3.5 miles. That is far smaller and geologically simpler than a region-wide mesh in Metro Vancouver.

Cost, safety, capacity, and whether autonomous vehicles mature as promised are all unanswered questions too. Treat the mesh as something worth studying, not something to count on.

The Waterfront and False Creek

The waterfront is Vancouver's greatest asset, and it is unusual because so much of it is public. The City counts 28 kilometres of uninterrupted seawall path from the Convention Centre to Spanish Banks. Few cities can say anything similar.

False Creek may be where the most remains to be done. The north shore's northeast corner is still parking lots and event space waiting for the viaducts to come down. The south shore's 1970s neighbourhood, False Creek South, is a much-loved mix of co-ops and leasehold housing on City land, and its long-term future has been the subject of years of discussion. The Flats to the east are filling in with institutions and employment space. My guess, and it is only a guess, is that within a generation False Creek could function as a continuous ring of neighbourhoods, parks and working waterfront around the inlet. Kayaks, ferries, festivals and dragon boats would matter as much as cars.

That future depends on how Vancouver handles the water itself.

Living With the Rising Sea

The same waterfront that makes Vancouver special makes it vulnerable. The City plans for about one metre of sea-level rise by 2100 and two metres by 2200. In False Creek, that means high tides overtopping parts of the seawall more and more often, especially in low spots such as Stamps Landing. Storms have already damaged sections of the Stanley Park seawall in recent years.

The City's response, set out in the False Creek Coastal Adaptation Plan (developed through the City's Sea2City process, which has now published its final report), is notably un-freeway-like. It doesn't rely on one big wall. It combines near-term shoreline protection with habitat benches, forest berms, "blue-green" corridors that give water somewhere to go, and over time, moving vulnerable development back from the edge.

Here I'm speculating. This could become the next defining Vancouver idea, after the seawall and the tower-and-podium block: a shoreline built to flood gracefully, with marshes and tidal parks absorbing storms that would otherwise batter concrete. It will be expensive. Some beloved places may change shape, and some that were saved from freeways may have to give ground to the sea. A city that turned down one kind of hard infrastructure in 1972 may again do best by turning down the easiest hard-engineering answer.

Stanley Park: Growing Old Growth on Purpose

One of the most important long-term projects in the city is happening at the scale of seedlings. This isn't logging. It is targeted removal of hazard trees, followed by a deliberately diverse replanting.

What happened. The hemlock looper is a native moth whose populations surge roughly every 15 years. It hit Stanley Park hard in an outbreak first identified in 2020. Drought and hard winters had already weakened the trees, and the outbreak lasted longer than usual. According to the City's Stanley Park forest management page, about 160,000 of the park's roughly 600,000 trees are dead or dying. Most of them are western hemlock, which the City notes had become over-represented after past logging and windstorms and is more prone than other conifers to disease, windthrow and stem breakage.

Why remove dead trees at all? The case rests on safety. The Park Board's impact and wildfire risk assessment expected dead trees to shed tops and branches, and to fail entirely, over the next two decades. As they did, dry fuel would build up on the forest floor. It classified 24 per cent of the park as high or extreme wildfire risk and another 60 per cent as moderate, and it warned that standing dead trees make emergency response harder and more dangerous. A 2024 Park Board report warned that an untreated forest could face "a devastating fire that would not only affect Stanley Park, but potentially the region."

Targeted, not wholesale. Only a fraction of the dead trees are being cut, mainly those near trails, roads and high-use areas. The City's rounded headline figure is about 14,000 trees removed. Its phase-by-phase totals add up to roughly 11,500. It also reports that 71 per cent of the park's forested areas have been treated. The Park Board's July 2025 update says no old-growth trees were removed and that work takes place from October to April, outside bird nesting season. Not everything cut is hauled away. Topped snags are left as wildlife habitat, and some felled trees stay on the ground as nurse logs and coarse woody debris. Fir, cedar and other cultural wood has gone to the Musqueam, Squamish and Tsleil-Waututh Nations for longhouse and ceremonial use. Most hemlock was sold or left on site. The January 2026 update notes that Park Board staff supported planting events with the Squamish and Tsleil-Waututh Nations. The final stage, which includes the trails around Beaver Lake, resumed this October and is expected to finish in spring 2027.

A more diverse forest. More than 75,000 seedlings have been planted so far, supported by a three-year donation from Western Forest Products. The 2024 Park Board report anticipated nearly 100,000 by the time the work is complete. The planting uses six native species: western redcedar, Douglas-fir, Sitka spruce, grand fir, red alder and Pacific yew. The goal, in the City's words, is a forest "more resilient to future insect outbreaks and climate change impacts." A mixed forest also spreads the risk. The next looper outbreak will find far less of the hemlock it prefers.

Why this could speed up the path to old growth. Portions of the forest were logged from the 1860s into the 1880s, according to the Park Board's restoration plan. Major windstorms in the 1930s, Typhoon Freda in 1962 and a storm in 2006 brought down more. Most of today's park is second growth. True old growth survives in one small remnant stand near Tunnel Trail and Pipeline Road, according to the Park Board's Forest Management Plan, along with veteran trees scattered through the park. Ecologically, old growth means more than old trees. The BC government describes large trees of varied sizes, species and ages, layered canopies with openings, and plenty of standing dead and decaying wood. A dense stand of same-aged hemlock is a long way from that.

Canopy gaps and mixed planting are a recognized way to close that distance. In western Washington, the U.S. Forest Service's Olympic Habitat Development Study tested "thinning with skips and gaps" in 40- to 70-year-old conifer stands to see whether it could speed up the development of late-successional structure. After 14 years, researchers found faster growth and longer crowns in mid-story trees and more recruitment of shade-tolerant species. They did not find consistent gains among the largest trees or in overall size diversity. The direction is encouraging, but the evidence is still accumulating.

How much time could this save? It's tempting to say this work could bring old growth back in about 300 years instead of 400 to 500. No source I found puts a number of years saved on active management, for Stanley Park or anywhere else, so treat that as a hypothesis. The best local benchmark comes from a Vancouver Island study in the same coastal western hemlock zone. It found that second-growth stands reached old-growth structural levels in as little as 112 years, but most took 200 to more than 250 years. Research on restoration thinning in the Pacific Northwest, such as this review, reports that it can speed up the development of old-growth characteristics. The honest summary: targeted removal and diverse planting push the forest in the right direction, and how many decades they save is uncertain. The structural qualities that make a forest feel ancient, including big cedars and firs, a layered canopy and a mix of species, depend heavily on what grows in the gaps now. Today's work is the main lever people have over that.

If Vancouver ever has cedars and firs of true old-growth scale again, they won't have arrived by accident. They will descend from seedlings planted in the gaps between 2024 and 2027.

Biodiversity: A City That Shares Its Edges

The freeway-free waterfront also turned out to be a gift to wildlife. Stanley Park is home to one of the largest urban great blue heron colonies in North America, according to the Park Board's Biodiversity Strategy. The herons have nested near the Park Board office at 2099 Beach Avenue since 2001, and in the park since at least 1921. In 2025, Stanley Park Ecology's monitoring report counted about 100 nests and recorded the colony's 25th consecutive year at that site. The Pacific great blue heron is blue-listed in BC, and the same strategy warns that many native birds, including several shorebirds and forest species, are declining.

The policy tools already exist. The 2016 Biodiversity Strategy guides restoration of natural areas. The 2025 Urban Forest Strategy aims to raise citywide tree canopy from 25 per cent to 30 per cent by 2050. The False Creek adaptation work includes habitat benches along the shoreline.

Speculation: if the city treats its seawall, its streams and the mixed-species forest of Stanley Park as one connected habitat network, Vancouver could become one of the few big North American cities where wildlife has more room in 2075 than it had in 1975.

Density Without Sprawl

Vancouver's lack of freeways has always depended on density. If people can't drive quickly into the core, enough of them have to live in or near it. Recent policy has pushed hard in that direction:

  • The Broadway Plan, approved in June 2022 and updated in December 2024, is a 30-year framework for housing, jobs and amenities along the subway corridor, roughly from Clark Drive to Vine Street and from 1st to 16th Avenue. The 2024 update raised heights and densities, partly to meet new provincial rules for transit-oriented areas. In October 2025 council approved new standardized low-, mid- and high-rise residential zones to speed up delivery.
  • Multiplex zoning, approved in September 2023 and in force from October 17, 2023, merged the city's many single-family zones into a single district and allowed multiplexes in low-density neighbourhoods that once allowed little more than a house and suites. Provincial legislation on small-scale multi-unit housing has since pushed in the same direction.

Together these mark a real shift. For decades, Vancouver's high-density downtown sat next to large low-density areas. The newer policies try to spread growth more gently across the city, especially along transit lines.

Speculation: if they work, the Vancouver of 2050 might look less like a cluster of downtown towers surrounded by houses and more like a broad, walkable carpet of mid-rise apartments and multiplexes with towers at transit nodes. That pattern can absorb growth without pushing it out to the edges of the region, which matters for affordability, emissions and the region's farmland and forests.

Indigenous Stewardship and Reconciliation

Some of the most consequential development in Vancouver's next half-century will be led by the Musqueam, Squamish and Tsleil-Waututh Nations. This is a profound change in who shapes the city, and it may matter more than anything planners do.

Sen̓áḵw, on Squamish Nation reserve land at the south end of the Burrard Bridge, broke ground on September 6, 2022. Its first tower began welcoming residents in late spring 2026. A second is expected to open this fall and a third by the end of the year. Those three towers on the west side of the bridge make up the first phase of about 1,400 rental homes. The full project is planned at 11 towers and more than 6,000 purpose-built rental homes, about a fifth of them below market, with priority for Squamish Nation members and then other Indigenous people. Because it sits on reserve land, it was not subject to City rezoning, and its height and density have drawn plenty of debate. The timing of later phases depends on financing and market conditions.

The MST Nations' partnerships are also reshaping two large sites:

  • ʔəy̓alməxʷ/Iy̓álmexw/Jericho Lands, about 90 acres in West Point Grey owned with the federal Canada Lands Company. Council approved a policy statement in January 2024 and an Official Development Plan on April 22, 2025, for roughly 13,000 homes over about 25 years. The first phased rezoning application was expected in late 2025 or early 2026, but I could not confirm it had been filed as of this writing. Reported targets put first homes around 2032.
  • The Heather Lands, 21 acres near West 33rd and Heather. Council approved a revised rezoning on March 31, 2026 for about 4,200 homes in 16 buildings of 4 to 46 storeys, with a cultural centre, childcare, a park and a francophone school. With provincial support through the Attainable Housing Initiative, most of the homes are intended to be sold below market prices. Some councillors questioned whether all of that provincial money will materialize, and the rezoning includes a fallback scenario in case it doesn't.

What follows is my reading, not a forecast: the deeper shift is that the Nations are planning for a time horizon measured in generations, on land they never ceded. Leasehold models, cultural centres, Indigenous place names and long-term rental housing all reflect a way of thinking about land as something to steward rather than sell. If Vancouver's identity in 1972 was "the city that stopped the freeway," its identity in 2072 might partly be "the city whose host Nations helped build it again."

The Risks

None of this is guaranteed, and the risks are real.

Affordability comes first. Vancouver's housing costs are among the highest in North America, and a walkable, transit-rich, waterfront city is exactly the kind of place people will pay a premium for. New supply helps, but there is a real danger that the city's best qualities become luxury goods.

Over-densification, or poorly done density, is the second risk. Council has reassessed the protected view cones to allow taller buildings, and proposals now reach 65 storeys. Height in itself is not the problem. The problem is towers that arrive without schools, parks, childcare, transit capacity or street life to match. The early success of "Vancouverism" depended on careful attention to the ground floor and the public realm, and that attention is harder to sustain at higher speed and larger scale.

Losing what makes it special is the subtlest risk. The things that set Vancouver apart, including the open waterfront, mountain views, quiet back lanes, small shops and the sense that neighbourhoods have a say, can wear away gradually. No single decision does it. A thousand individually reasonable ones can.

Pressure on the region is the last. A Vancouver that stays freeway-free still depends on Highway 1, the bridges, and a ring of fast-growing suburbs such as Burnaby and Surrey that carry much of the region's growth and car traffic. The city's virtues are partly paid for elsewhere, and a fair future has to recognize that.

An Opportunity, Not an Inheritance

Speculating one last time: I think Vancouver's advantages are real, and few cities on this continent could do what Vancouver can over the next fifty years. It can finish its subway, open up its last downtown waterfront, build a shoreline that works with the sea, grow up instead of out, and do much of it in genuine partnership with the Nations whose land it is.

Advantages don't defend themselves, though. The freeway wasn't stopped by geography or luck alone. Neighbours sat at kitchen tables, knocked on doors and showed up at hearings that ran past midnight. The next fifty years will call for the same kind of attention, applied to rent, transit, the shoreline and who gets to live here. The city avoided an old mistake. Whether it makes the most of that will depend on the same habits of attention and participation.
