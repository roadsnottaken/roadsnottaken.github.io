Before the city · thousands of years

This was home long before it was a city.

xʷməθkʷəy̓əm (Musqueam) · Sḵwx̱wú7mesh (Squamish) · səlilwətaɬ (Tsleil-Waututh)

People of the xʷməθkʷəy̓əm, Sḵwx̱wú7mesh and səlilwətaɬ Nations have lived here for thousands of years. At c̓əsnaʔəm, by the Fraser in today's Marpole, a village was first occupied 4,000 to 5,000 years ago (City of Vancouver; Museum of Anthropology).

On Burrard Inlet, təmtəmíxʷtən at Belcarra goes back to about 1550 BCE, and its chum salmon fishery lasted some 2,500 years (PLOS One; UBC). At X̱wáy̓x̱way, now Lumberman's Arch, a shell midden about 8 feet deep was dug up to surface the first park road in 1888 (Matthews).

Smallpox, then the reserve system, cut these communities hard. In 1913 families at Sen̓áḵw were forcibly removed by barge, and in 2003 the courts returned part of the reserve (Squamish Nation). The three Nations live and govern here today.

Move people, not lanes.

In 1972 the city turned down a freeway crossing. Now the question is how far trains, boats and buses should reach, and who gets to ride them.

Leashed dogs on transit

Lap carriers only: the rule for pets on SkyTrain and buses

On TransLink, pets must ride in small, hand-held, fully enclosed carriers, and only service dogs are exempt. The SeaBus has kennels, but only two per sailing. TransLink reviewed the policy in 2018 and kept it.

What could changeToronto's TTC lets leashed dogs ride outside weekday rush hours. San Francisco's Muni allows one leashed, muzzled dog per vehicle off-peak. Seattle-area King County Metro lets leashed dogs ride for the price of a fare. On False Creek, the private Aquabus already welcomes leashed dogs. Other cities let leashed dogs ride off-peak. Vancouver could too.

Transit: The Broadway Subway and Beyond

The single biggest near-term change is underground. The Broadway Subway is a 5.7-kilometre extension of the Millennium Line from VCC–Clark to Arbutus Street, with six new underground stations. Its trackwork was finished in September 2026, train testing began in May, and it is scheduled to open for passenger service in fall 2027. The province says it will have three times the capacity of the 99 B-Line it partly replaces, and the 99 has been one of the most crowded bus routes in the region.

The obvious next step is UBC. A roughly seven-kilometre extension from Arbutus to the university, with stations along the route including one planned for the Jericho Lands, is still in planning and design under the provincial Ministry of Transportation and Transit. The federal and provincial governments have funded that planning work, but as of this autumn there is no full construction funding commitment. Across the inlet, TransLink is studying rapid transit to the North Shore as part of a Burrard Inlet Rapid Transit program that could eventually include a dedicated transit crossing. Half a century after Vancouverites killed a freeway tunnel near Brockton Point, the new third-crossing debate is about trains and buses instead of lanes into downtown.

A Connected Region

Vancouver doesn't stand alone. The city's freeway-free core works because the whole region is tied together by transit, and that network is still growing.

The backbone already exists. The Expo Line runs from Waterfront through Burnaby and New Westminster to Surrey. The Millennium Line serves Burnaby and the Tri-Cities as far as Lafarge Lake–Douglas in Coquitlam. The Canada Line connects downtown to Richmond and YVR. The 16-kilometre Surrey–Langley extension, recently named the Dave Barrett Extension, is under construction along Fraser Highway. It will have eight stations and is expected to open in late 2029. Once it opens, Langley City Centre will have a direct SkyTrain connection to downtown Vancouver.

From the history essay

The Resident Revolt

Two currents of opposition came together. The first was citywide. On June 1, 1967, council moved to accept the study's recommendation for a first freeway segment through Chinatown. Chinese merchants, local architects and Alderman Harry Rankin forced public hearings, and those hearings revealed that the consultants had never been asked to consider alternatives to the route. UBC geographer Walter Hardwick had been challenging the freeway studies in the press since 1964. Other critics included Peter Oberlander, head of UBC's planning school, and the engineer Setty Pendakur.

The "Great Freeway Debate" came to a head on November 23, 1967. More than 500 people crowded into city hall, and The Province's Bud Elsie called it the "wildest, most stormy meeting ever seen at city hall." The hearing continued at Eric Hamber Secondary, where Oberlander resigned as chair of the Town Planning Commission. "Planning is too serious a matter to leave solely to professional civic officials," he said. The Chinatown route was dropped in 1968.

02 · Shore, sea and pipes

The water is the city.

Aging pipes, closed beaches and a rising sea will decide how much of the shoreline everyone shares stays usable.

The case for a waterfront that's open and clean

5 city beaches under E. coli no-swim advisories in July 2025

In July 2025, English Bay, Sunset, Kitsilano, Second and Third beaches were all under no-swimming advisories for E. coli. False Creek is rated not suitable for swimming, eight years after council voted to make it swimmable. A two-year water study will report in 2027.

Why it mattersOverflows from the old combined pipes and rising seas will decide how much of the shoreline people can actually use. The City's own stated goal is "continued access to treasured coastal areas."

Sources CBC, Jul 18, 2025 · Vancouver Sun, Sep 25, 2025 · City: Adapting to sea level rise

The waterfront meets the sea

1 m of sea-level rise planned for by 2100

The seawall runs 28 km without a break. The City plans for about 1 m of sea-level rise by 2100, which would put 13 km² of land in the floodplain. Its False Creek plan uses habitat benches, forest berms and "blue-green" corridors, not one big wall.

Aging sewers, overflowing into the water

536 km of combined sewers still to separate

At the end of 2023, 57.8% of sewer mains were separated. The rest carry sewage and rainwater together and can overflow into False Creek and Burrard Inlet in heavy rain. The regional deadline to end overflows is 2050. The City estimated in 2022 that meeting it would take about $99 million a year, up from about $36 million.

Last timeVancouver began replacing its combined pipes with separate ones in the 1970s. Half a century later, the job is a little over half done.

Sources City: Healthy Waters key directions (2025) · City: Healthy Waters phase 1 · Metro Vancouver liquid waste plan

Water mains reaching their end

22% of the City's waterworks assets are in poor condition

The City projects 26% within a decade as pipes reach the end of their lives. It expects more main breaks and leaks, and it raised its yearly renewal rate from 0.9% to 1.1% in the 2023–2026 capital plan.

Why it mattersThe City says the capacity of its pipes is a key uncertainty for population growth and new housing.

The Waterfront and False Creek

The waterfront is Vancouver's greatest asset, and it is unusual because so much of it is public. The City counts 28 kilometres of uninterrupted seawall path from the Convention Centre to Spanish Banks. Few cities can say anything similar.

False Creek may be where the most remains to be done. The north shore's northeast corner is still parking lots and event space waiting for the viaducts to come down. The south shore's 1970s neighbourhood, False Creek South, is a much-loved mix of co-ops and leasehold housing on City land, and its long-term future has been the subject of years of discussion. The Flats to the east are filling in with institutions and employment space. My guess, and it is only a guess, is that within a generation False Creek could function as a continuous ring of neighbourhoods, parks and working waterfront around the inlet. Kayaks, ferries, festivals and dragon boats would matter as much as cars.

Living With the Rising Sea

The same waterfront that makes Vancouver special makes it vulnerable. The City plans for about one metre of sea-level rise by 2100 and two metres by 2200. In False Creek, that means high tides overtopping parts of the seawall more and more often, especially in low spots such as Stamps Landing. Storms have already damaged sections of the Stanley Park seawall in recent years.

The City's response, set out in the False Creek Coastal Adaptation Plan (developed through the City's Sea2City process, which has now published its final report), is notably un-freeway-like. It doesn't rely on one big wall. It combines near-term shoreline protection with habitat benches, forest berms, "blue-green" corridors that give water somewhere to go, and over time, moving vulnerable development back from the edge.

From the history essay

What Grew in the Freeway's Place

Without expressways emptying downtown at five o'clock, Vancouver had both the motive and the room to make living downtown appealing. The result became known as Vancouverism: slim residential towers on low podiums of townhouses and shops, which keep the street lively while leaving room for light and views.

Waterfront land the freeway would have taken became neighbourhoods. False Creek South was planned by the City in 1974 and built in phases from 1975, replacing industry with co-ops and mixed-income housing. After Expo 86 the province sold the fair site to Concord Pacific in 1988, and False Creek North filled in over the following two decades. Coal Harbour went up on former railway and industrial land along Burrard Inlet. The seawall links all of it. It was begun around Stanley Park in 1917, the loop around the park was completed in 1980, and today the City counts 28 kilometres of uninterrupted waterfront path from the Convention Centre to Spanish Banks.

Finishing the Story: The Viaducts and Northeast False Creek

The Georgia and Dunsmuir viaducts, opened in 1971 and 1972, are the only pieces of the old freeway plan ever built. Council voted on October 27, 2015 to remove them, and in 2018 it approved the Northeast False Creek Plan to replace them. That plan calls for an at-grade street network linking downtown to the water, 32 acres of new and renewed parks and open space, about 1,800 units of social housing, and a neighbourhood shaped by reconciliation with local First Nations and cultural redress for the Black and Chinese Canadian communities. Part of that redress is a City memorandum of understanding with the Hogan's Alley Society for the 898 Main Street block. Hogan's Alley, the heart of Vancouver's Black community, was largely erased to build the viaducts. The MOU envisions housing and a cultural centre there.

The honest status report is less inspiring. As of this month the viaducts are still standing, and the City says their removal timeline "is still uncertain." Funding depends heavily on redevelopment by landowners such as Concord Pacific, and a weak condo market and rising construction costs have stalled it. In 2018 the City estimated the street-network changes at $438 million, and staff have said that figure needs to be reassessed. In January 2025 Concord unveiled a revised concept called Concord Landing: about 5,000 homes in 12 towers, some as tall as 65 storeys, around a pedestrian village and a completed stretch of seawall. City officials have since been meeting regularly with Concord and talking with senior governments about financing. Meanwhile crews began replacing expansion joints on the Georgia Viaduct on October 2, with work expected to last until spring 2027. The demolition plan is still alive, but nobody has scheduled it.

Stanley Park: Growing Old Growth on Purpose

One of the most important long-term projects in the city is happening at the scale of seedlings. This isn't logging. It is targeted removal of hazard trees, followed by a deliberately diverse replanting.

What happened. The hemlock looper is a native moth whose populations surge roughly every 15 years. It hit Stanley Park hard in an outbreak first identified in 2020. Drought and hard winters had already weakened the trees, and the outbreak lasted longer than usual. According to the City's Stanley Park forest management page, about 160,000 of the park's roughly 600,000 trees are dead or dying. Most of them are western hemlock, which the City notes had become over-represented after past logging and windstorms and is more prone than other conifers to disease, windthrow and stem breakage.

How the plan was drawn, and stopped

From the history essay

Plans on Paper: From Pleasure Drives to a Master Freeway Plan

Vancouver's first comprehensive plan came from the St. Louis firm Harland Bartholomew and Associates. It was prepared in 1928 and revised in 1929 after Point Grey and South Vancouver amalgamated with the city. The City of Vancouver Archives describes a plan that covered transportation, parks, schools and zoning. Its major-street maps marked streets to be widened, new connections and "pleasure drives." That is about as much detail as the surviving summaries give. It was a plan of wider arterials and scenic routes, not high-speed parkways, and it was influential even though only part of it was adopted. Follow-up reports came in 1944–48, but their road proposals are thinly documented.

The freeway era really started in the 1950s. Gerald Sutton-Brown was an English-trained planner who became the city's first director of planning and later one of its two powerful appointed commissioners. He chaired a technical committee whose 1959 report became, in effect, a master freeway plan. In 1964 the Stanford Research Institute and Wilbur Smith and Associates updated it. In 1965 voters approved money to replace the shaky 1915 Georgia Viaduct over the rail yards east of downtown, with little public discussion of what the new structure might eventually connect to.

From the history essay

The Viaducts: A Fragment Left Standing

The viaducts got built even as the network they were meant to serve collapsed. The Georgia structure opened on June 28, 1971, and the Dunsmuir side on January 9, 1972, with protesters trying to block the mayor's car. They are the only part of the freeway system ever built.

On October 27, 2015, council voted to remove them. The 2018 Northeast False Creek Plan would replace them with an at-grade street network, parks and housing, along with redress commitments that include a City memorandum of understanding with the Hogan's Alley Society for the 898 Main Street block. As of autumn 2026 the viaducts are still standing. Demolition was first targeted for 2019 and has slipped ever since. Funding depends on redevelopment by landowners such as Concord Pacific, and that has stalled with a weak condo market and rising construction costs. The City is exploring financing from senior governments. In the meantime it is repairing them instead: expansion-joint replacement on the Georgia Viaduct began this October and is expected to run into spring 2027. The City says the removal timeline "is still uncertain," and CBC reports that advocates are trying to make the viaducts an issue in the October 17 civic election.

Urban Renewal in Strathcona and Hogan's Alley

The freeway followed years of "urban renewal" aimed at Strathcona, the city's oldest residential neighbourhood. Its residents included Chinese, Italian, Japanese, Ukrainian and Black families, among many others.

The groundwork went back decades. A 1931 bylaw zoned much of the East End industrial. In 1950 a study led by Leonard Marsh called for slum clearance. The City's 1957 redevelopment study listed Strathcona among the areas most in need of redevelopment. The City of Vancouver now acknowledges that through the 1950s and 1960s it stopped granting building permits in the area and let its infrastructure decline. History essay

The Roads Not Taken: How Vancouver Grew Up Without Freeways

Drive into downtown Vancouver from the east and the moment comes. Highway 1 bends away at the city's edge, and instead of a ramp lifting you onto an elevated expressway, you end up on Hastings or Main at a red light, next to a bus and a cyclist. Visitors from Seattle or Toronto can take it for an oversight. It was actually the result of decisions, fights and a bit of luck, and it left Vancouver as the only major city in North America without a freeway running into its core.

Plans on Paper: From Pleasure Drives to a Master Freeway Plan

Vancouver's first comprehensive plan came from the St. Louis firm Harland Bartholomew and Associates. It was prepared in 1928 and revised in 1929 after Point Grey and South Vancouver amalgamated with the city. The City of Vancouver Archives describes a plan that covered transportation, parks, schools and zoning. Its major-street maps marked streets to be widened, new connections and "pleasure drives." That is about as much detail as the surviving summaries give. It was a plan of wider arterials and scenic routes, not high-speed parkways, and it was influential even though only part of it was adopted. Follow-up reports came in 1944–48, but their road proposals are thinly documented.

The freeway era really started in the 1950s. Gerald Sutton-Brown was an English-trained planner who became the city's first director of planning and later one of its two powerful appointed commissioners. He chaired a technical committee whose 1959 report became, in effect, a master freeway plan. In 1964 the Stanford Research Institute and Wilbur Smith and Associates updated it. In 1965 voters approved money to replace the shaky 1915 Georgia Viaduct over the rail yards east of downtown, with little public discussion of what the new structure might eventually connect to.

Urban Renewal in Strathcona and Hogan's Alley

The freeway followed years of "urban renewal" aimed at Strathcona, the city's oldest residential neighbourhood. Its residents included Chinese, Italian, Japanese, Ukrainian and Black families, among many others.

The groundwork went back decades. A 1931 bylaw zoned much of the East End industrial. In 1950 a study led by Leonard Marsh called for slum clearance. The City's 1957 redevelopment study listed Strathcona among the areas most in need of redevelopment. The City of Vancouver now acknowledges that through the 1950s and 1960s it stopped granting building permits in the area and let its infrastructure decline. Clearance began around 1960 with funding shared by all three levels of government, over the protests of Chinese property owners. Whole blocks came down for the MacLean Park towers and the Raymur public housing project, now Stamps Place. Displacement figures vary by source, but thousands of people were moved across the phases. In 1971 the Militant Mothers of Raymur blockaded the rail line their children crossed to school and won a Keefer Street overpass.

The Resident Revolt

Two currents of opposition came together. The first was citywide. On June 1, 1967, council moved to accept the study's recommendation for a first freeway segment through Chinatown. Chinese merchants, local architects and Alderman Harry Rankin forced public hearings, and those hearings revealed that the consultants had never been asked to consider alternatives to the route. UBC geographer Walter Hardwick had been challenging the freeway studies in the press since 1964. Other critics included Peter Oberlander, head of UBC's planning school, and the engineer Setty Pendakur.

The "Great Freeway Debate" came to a head on November 23, 1967. More than 500 people crowded into city hall, and The Province's Bud Elsie called it the "wildest, most stormy meeting ever seen at city hall." The hearing continued at Eric Hamber Secondary, where Oberlander resigned as chair of the Town Planning Commission. "Planning is too serious a matter to leave solely to professional civic officials," he said. The Chinatown route was dropped in 1968.

The Viaducts: A Fragment Left Standing

The viaducts got built even as the network they were meant to serve collapsed. The Georgia structure opened on June 28, 1971, and the Dunsmuir side on January 9, 1972, with protesters trying to block the mayor's car. They are the only part of the freeway system ever built.

On October 27, 2015, council voted to remove them. The 2018 Northeast False Creek Plan would replace them with an at-grade street network, parks and housing, along with redress commitments that include a City memorandum of understanding with the Hogan's Alley Society for the 898 Main Street block. As of autumn 2026 the viaducts are still standing. Demolition was first targeted for 2019 and has slipped ever since. Funding depends on redevelopment by landowners such as Concord Pacific, and that has stalled with a weak condo market and rising construction costs. The City is exploring financing from senior governments. In the meantime it is repairing them instead: expansion-joint replacement on the Georgia Viaduct began this October and is expected to run into spring 2027. The City says the removal timeline "is still uncertain," and CBC reports that advocates are trying to make the viaducts an issue in the October 17 civic election.

What Grew in the Freeway's Place

Without expressways emptying downtown at five o'clock, Vancouver had both the motive and the room to make living downtown appealing. The result became known as Vancouverism: slim residential towers on low podiums of townhouses and shops, which keep the street lively while leaving room for light and views.

Waterfront land the freeway would have taken became neighbourhoods. False Creek South was planned by the City in 1974 and built in phases from 1975, replacing industry with co-ops and mixed-income housing. After Expo 86 the province sold the fair site to Concord Pacific in 1988, and False Creek North filled in over the following two decades. Coal Harbour went up on former railway and industrial land along Burrard Inlet. The seawall links all of it. It was begun around Stanley Park in 1917, the loop around the park was completed in 1980, and today the City counts 28 kilometres of uninterrupted waterfront path from the Convention Centre to Spanish Banks.

Economic and Social Effects: Gains and Trade-offs

The gains are easy to see. The downtown peninsula and its nearby neighbourhoods are among the most walkable places in Canada. A large share of residents walk, cycle or take transit, and everyday active travel is good for public health. The seawall, Stanley Park, Granville Island and the open waterfront are central to tourism.

The trade-offs are real too. A compact, water-bound core with limited road capacity makes central land scarce and expensive, and Vancouver's housing costs are among the highest in North America. The lack of freeways is only one cause among many, along with geography, outside investment, zoning that long protected single-family neighbourhoods, and strong demand. Crosstown trips and bridge approaches can be slow, and busy transit routes, the 99 B-Line on Broadway above all, have been crowded for years.

The Next Fifty Years: Some Speculation

What follows is educated guesswork, not prediction.

Part of the near future is already under construction. The Broadway Subway, a 5.7-kilometre Millennium Line extension to Arbutus with six underground stations, finished its trackwork in September 2026 and is scheduled to open in fall 2027. Extending it to UBC is still in planning, without full funding committed. The George Massey Tunnel is to be replaced by an eight-lane immersed-tube tunnel. The province now projects about $8.5 billion and completion in September 2031. The idea of a new Burrard Inlet crossing has come back too, but TransLink is now studying rapid transit to the North Shore. The contrast with 1972 is the point: the debate is about moving people, not adding lanes into downtown.

The City That Kept Its Options Open: Vancouver's Next Fifty Years

Most big North American cities spend their future paying for their past. Seattle spent years and a lot of money replacing the Alaskan Way Viaduct with a tunnel. Boston paid for the Big Dig. Toronto is still arguing over the Gardiner. A great deal of urban ambition on this continent goes into undoing what the expressway era built.

Finishing the Story: The Viaducts and Northeast False Creek

The Georgia and Dunsmuir viaducts, opened in 1971 and 1972, are the only pieces of the old freeway plan ever built. Council voted on October 27, 2015 to remove them, and in 2018 it approved the Northeast False Creek Plan to replace them. That plan calls for an at-grade street network linking downtown to the water, 32 acres of new and renewed parks and open space, about 1,800 units of social housing, and a neighbourhood shaped by reconciliation with local First Nations and cultural redress for the Black and Chinese Canadian communities. Part of that redress is a City memorandum of understanding with the Hogan's Alley Society for the 898 Main Street block. Hogan's Alley, the heart of Vancouver's Black community, was largely erased to build the viaducts. The MOU envisions housing and a cultural centre there.

The honest status report is less inspiring. As of this month the viaducts are still standing, and the City says their removal timeline "is still uncertain." Funding depends heavily on redevelopment by landowners such as Concord Pacific, and a weak condo market and rising construction costs have stalled it. In 2018 the City estimated the street-network changes at $438 million, and staff have said that figure needs to be reassessed. In January 2025 Concord unveiled a revised concept called Concord Landing: about 5,000 homes in 12 towers, some as tall as 65 storeys, around a pedestrian village and a completed stretch of seawall. City officials have since been meeting regularly with Concord and talking with senior governments about financing. Meanwhile crews began replacing expansion joints on the Georgia Viaduct on October 2, with work expected to last until spring 2027. The demolition plan is still alive, but nobody has scheduled it.

Transit: The Broadway Subway and Beyond

The single biggest near-term change is underground. The Broadway Subway is a 5.7-kilometre extension of the Millennium Line from VCC–Clark to Arbutus Street, with six new underground stations. Its trackwork was finished in September 2026, train testing began in May, and it is scheduled to open for passenger service in fall 2027. The province says it will have three times the capacity of the 99 B-Line it partly replaces, and the 99 has been one of the most crowded bus routes in the region.

The obvious next step is UBC. A roughly seven-kilometre extension from Arbutus to the university, with stations along the route including one planned for the Jericho Lands, is still in planning and design under the provincial Ministry of Transportation and Transit. The federal and provincial governments have funded that planning work, but as of this autumn there is no full construction funding commitment. Across the inlet, TransLink is studying rapid transit to the North Shore as part of a Burrard Inlet Rapid Transit program that could eventually include a dedicated transit crossing. Half a century after Vancouverites killed a freeway tunnel near Brockton Point, the new third-crossing debate is about trains and buses instead of lanes into downtown.

A Connected Region

Vancouver doesn't stand alone. The city's freeway-free core works because the whole region is tied together by transit, and that network is still growing.

The backbone already exists. The Expo Line runs from Waterfront through Burnaby and New Westminster to Surrey. The Millennium Line serves Burnaby and the Tri-Cities as far as Lafarge Lake–Douglas in Coquitlam. The Canada Line connects downtown to Richmond and YVR. The 16-kilometre Surrey–Langley extension, recently named the Dave Barrett Extension, is under construction along Fraser Highway. It will have eight stations and is expected to open in late 2029. Once it opens, Langley City Centre will have a direct SkyTrain connection to downtown Vancouver.

The Waterfront and False Creek

The waterfront is Vancouver's greatest asset, and it is unusual because so much of it is public. The City counts 28 kilometres of uninterrupted seawall path from the Convention Centre to Spanish Banks. Few cities can say anything similar.

False Creek may be where the most remains to be done. The north shore's northeast corner is still parking lots and event space waiting for the viaducts to come down. The south shore's 1970s neighbourhood, False Creek South, is a much-loved mix of co-ops and leasehold housing on City land, and its long-term future has been the subject of years of discussion. The Flats to the east are filling in with institutions and employment space. My guess, and it is only a guess, is that within a generation False Creek could function as a continuous ring of neighbourhoods, parks and working waterfront around the inlet. Kayaks, ferries, festivals and dragon boats would matter as much as cars.

Living With the Rising Sea

The same waterfront that makes Vancouver special makes it vulnerable. The City plans for about one metre of sea-level rise by 2100 and two metres by 2200. In False Creek, that means high tides overtopping parts of the seawall more and more often, especially in low spots such as Stamps Landing. Storms have already damaged sections of the Stanley Park seawall in recent years.

The City's response, set out in the False Creek Coastal Adaptation Plan (developed through the City's Sea2City process, which has now published its final report), is notably un-freeway-like. It doesn't rely on one big wall. It combines near-term shoreline protection with habitat benches, forest berms, "blue-green" corridors that give water somewhere to go, and over time, moving vulnerable development back from the edge.

Stanley Park: Growing Old Growth on Purpose

One of the most important long-term projects in the city is happening at the scale of seedlings. This isn't logging. It is targeted removal of hazard trees, followed by a deliberately diverse replanting.

What happened. The hemlock looper is a native moth whose populations surge roughly every 15 years. It hit Stanley Park hard in an outbreak first identified in 2020. Drought and hard winters had already weakened the trees, and the outbreak lasted longer than usual. According to the City's Stanley Park forest management page, about 160,000 of the park's roughly 600,000 trees are dead or dying. Most of them are western hemlock, which the City notes had become over-represented after past logging and windstorms and is more prone than other conifers to disease, windthrow and stem breakage.

Biodiversity: A City That Shares Its Edges

The freeway-free waterfront also turned out to be a gift to wildlife. Stanley Park is home to one of the largest urban great blue heron colonies in North America, according to the Park Board's Biodiversity Strategy. The herons have nested near the Park Board office at 2099 Beach Avenue since 2001, and in the park since at least 1921. In 2025, Stanley Park Ecology's monitoring report counted about 100 nests and recorded the colony's 25th consecutive year at that site. The Pacific great blue heron is blue-listed in BC, and the same strategy warns that many native birds, including several shorebirds and forest species, are declining.

The policy tools already exist. The 2016 Biodiversity Strategy guides restoration of natural areas. The 2025 Urban Forest Strategy aims to raise citywide tree canopy from 25 per cent to 30 per cent by 2050. The False Creek adaptation work includes habitat benches along the shoreline.

Indigenous Stewardship and Reconciliation

Some of the most consequential development in Vancouver's next half-century will be led by the Musqueam, Squamish and Tsleil-Waututh Nations. This is a profound change in who shapes the city, and it may matter more than anything planners do.

Sen̓áḵw, on Squamish Nation reserve land at the south end of the Burrard Bridge, broke ground on September 6, 2022. Its first tower began welcoming residents in late spring 2026. A second is expected to open this fall and a third by the end of the year. Those three towers on the west side of the bridge make up the first phase of about 1,400 rental homes. The full project is planned at 11 towers and more than 6,000 purpose-built rental homes, about a fifth of them below market, with priority for Squamish Nation members and then other Indigenous people. Because it sits on reserve land, it was not subject to City rezoning, and its height and density have drawn plenty of debate. The timing of later phases depends on financing and market conditions.

The Risks

None of this is guaranteed, and the risks are real.

Affordability comes first. Vancouver's housing costs are among the highest in North America, and a walkable, transit-rich, waterfront city is exactly the kind of place people will pay a premium for. New supply helps, but there is a real danger that the city's best qualities become luxury goods.

An Opportunity, Not an Inheritance

Speculating one last time: I think Vancouver's advantages are real, and few cities on this continent could do what Vancouver can over the next fifty years. It can finish its subway, open up its last downtown waterfront, build a shoreline that works with the sea, grow up instead of out, and do much of it in genuine partnership with the Nations whose land it is.

Advantages don't defend themselves, though. The freeway wasn't stopped by geography or luck alone. Neighbours sat at kitchen tables, knocked on doors and showed up at hearings that ran past midnight. The next fifty years will call for the same kind of attention, applied to rent, transit, the shoreline and who gets to live here. The city avoided an old mistake. Whether it makes the most of that will depend on the same habits of attention and participation.
