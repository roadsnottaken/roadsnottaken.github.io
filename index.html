The Roads Not Taken

Vancouver is the only major city in North America without a freeway running into its core. That came from decisions, fights and a bit of luck. Here is how it happened, and what the city could do with the room it kept.

1. The plan: a freeway through the core

From the 1959 master plan to the 1967 route through Chinatown, and the viaducts that still stand.

The 1967 Vancouver Transportation Study showed an elevated waterfront freeway swinging through Gastown's Maple Tree Square and the heart of Chinatown at Carrall Street, then east along Prior Street toward Highway 1. A third crossing to the North Shore would land near Brockton Point, at the edge of Stanley Park. The new Georgia Viaduct would carry the traffic into downtown.

    2. Who stopped it, and how

    Residents, merchants and experts, using hearings, organizing and persistence.

    1. They demanded a hearing. In 1967 Chinese merchants, local architects and Alderman Harry Rankin forced public hearings. The hearings showed the consultants had never been asked to consider alternatives.
    2. They showed up. On November 23, 1967, more than 500 people crowded into city hall. The Chinatown route was dropped in 1968.
    3. They organized block by block. SPOTA started at a kitchen table at 658 Keefer Street and grew to more than 600 members. Block captains carried news house to house, and the group recruited non-Chinese neighbours too.
    4. They followed the money. After Mary Chan met federal minister Paul Hellyer, Ottawa froze urban-renewal funding, and the next phase of clearance couldn't be paid for.
    5. They offered an alternative. SPOTA argued renewal money should repair homes instead. That became a model for a national program and won the Vincent Massey Award in 1975.
    6. They kept going. By February 1972 a petition against the third crossing had more than 21,000 signatures. By the end of 1972 the crossing and its feeder freeways were dead.

    “Planning is too serious a matter to leave solely to professional civic officials.”

    Peter Oberlander, resigning as chair of the Town Planning Commission, 1967 (quoted in the history essay)

    What survived, and what didn't

    Chinatown

    The 1967 study's elevated waterfront freeway would have swung through Gastown's Maple Tree Square and the heart of Chinatown at Carrall Street. Chinese merchants, local architects and Alderman Harry Rankin forced public hearings, and the Chinatown route was dropped in 1968. Chinatown survived.

    Strathcona

    The city's oldest residential neighbourhood, home to Chinese, Italian, Japanese, Ukrainian and Black families, was targeted for "urban renewal". Residents formed SPOTA in 1968 at the kitchen table of Mary Lee Chan and Walter Chan, 658 Keefer Street. They got federal clearance money frozen and argued for repairing homes instead of bulldozing them. The rehabilitation won the Vincent Massey Award in 1975.

    Hogan's Alley

    Park Lane and the blocks around it were the heart of Vancouver's Black community: the Fountain Chapel, chicken houses like Vie's, and Nora Hendrix, Jimi Hendrix's grandmother. From 1967 the City began levelling its western half for the viaduct project. The City says the displacement was "finalized by the construction of the Georgia and Dunsmuir viaducts in 1972." Very little of Black Strathcona survived. The Northeast False Creek Plan includes a City MOU with the Hogan's Alley Society for the 898 Main Street block.

    Why it matters: Because the freeway never came, the land and money went somewhere else. What we gained →

    3. What we gained, and what it cost

    Without expressways emptying downtown at five o'clock, the city had both a reason and the room to make downtown liveable.

    People live downtown

    "Vancouverism" means slim towers on low podiums of townhouses and shops, which keep the street lively while leaving light and views.

    The waterfront became neighbourhoods

    False Creek South was planned in 1974 with co-ops and mixed-income housing. False Creek North filled in after 1988, and Coal Harbour went up on former rail land. The seawall now gives 28 km of uninterrupted path from the Convention Centre to Spanish Banks.

    Transit carried the load

    The Expo Line opened in December 1985. The 1997 Transportation Plan put walking first, then cycling, transit and goods movement, with private cars last. West End traffic calming began in 1973–74.

    The trade-offs are real

    Central land is scarce and expensive, and housing costs are among the highest in North America (the lack of freeways is only one cause). Crosstown trips are slow, the 99 B-Line has been crowded for years, and much of the growth and traffic moved to the suburbs.

    For comparison: Seattle replaced its Alaskan Way Viaduct with a tunnel, Boston buried its Central Artery in the Big Dig, and San Francisco's Embarcadero Freeway came down in 1991. Vancouver got many of the same benefits without building a freeway and then paying to remove it.

    Why it matters: These benefits weren't luck, and keeping them isn't automatic. The lesson →

    4. The lesson

    The freeway wasn't stopped by geography or luck alone.

    “The people of Strathcona in 1968 weren't arguing urban theory. They were defending their homes and their neighbours, and their right to be consulted.”

    History essay

    Vancouver can stay freeway-free "if residents treat it as a choice that has to be made again and again rather than a legacy that maintains itself."

    The same habits can be used on today's questions. The difference from 1972 is the point: the new Burrard Inlet crossing debate is about moving people, not adding lanes into downtown.

    5. Applying the lesson: transit and the waterfront

    The same choice comes up again: move people, keep the waterfront public, and work with the sea instead of fighting it.

    Some of this is on the public record. Some is speculation, and it is labelled Speculative.

    Viaducts & Northeast False Creek

    Council voted to remove the viaducts on October 27, 2015. The 2018 plan calls for an at-grade street network, 32 acres of new and renewed parks and open space, and about 1,800 social housing units. As of October 2026 the viaducts are still standing, and the City says the removal timeline "is still uncertain." Expansion-joint work on the Georgia Viaduct started October 2 and is expected to run until spring 2027.

    Broadway Subway

    A 5.7 km Millennium Line extension from VCC–Clark to Arbutus with six underground stations. Trackwork was finished in September 2026, and it is scheduled to open in fall 2027. The UBC extension is still in planning, and construction funding has not been committed.

    Living with the sea

    The City plans for about 1 m of sea-level rise by 2100 and 2 m by 2200. The False Creek Coastal Adaptation Plan combines shoreline protection, habitat benches, forest berms and "blue-green" corridors instead of relying on one big wall.

    “Half a century after Vancouverites killed a freeway tunnel near Brockton Point, the new third-crossing debate is about trains and buses instead of lanes into downtown.”

    Future essay

    A tunnel mesh for transit, not cars Speculative

    Picture many small tunnels for shared, electric, automated vehicles, with SkyTrain as the backbone, leaving the surface for people. Nobody has proposed this. Soft delta soils, earthquakes and water crossings are serious obstacles. In the essay's words: "Treat the mesh as something worth studying, not something to count on."

    The last downtown waterfront

    Northeast False Creek could become streets, 32 acres of parks and about 1,800 social housing units once the viaducts come down. That would include redress for Hogan's Alley at 898 Main Street. Funding is still unresolved.

    A shoreline built to flood gracefully Partly speculative

    The City plans for about 1 m of sea-level rise by 2100. Its False Creek plan uses habitat benches, forest berms and blue-green corridors instead of one big wall. That is the same instinct as 1972: turn down the easiest hard-engineering answer.

    5. Applying the lesson: caring for Stanley Park

    A third crossing once aimed at the park's edge. Today the work is about its forest, and the choices being made now will show for centuries.

    “If Vancouver ever has cedars and firs of true old-growth scale again, they won't have arrived by accident.”

    Why the trees are dying

    The hemlock looper is a native moth, and its outbreaks come about every 15 years. Drought and hard winters had already left the trees vulnerable. This outbreak lasted longer than the usual two years. Most of the damage is to western hemlock, with some Douglas fir and grand fir. More than 32% of the forest was killed or defoliated. [FM] [R24]

    Removal phases
    • Phase 1 (Oct 2023 to Apr 2024): 7,201 trees across 67 ha
    • Phase 2 (Oct to Dec 2024): 1,329 trees across 41 ha
    • Phase 3, first stage (Jan to Apr 2026): 2,982 trees across 34 ha
    • Phase 3, final stage: started Oct 13, 2026 near Thompson Trail, including the interior trails around Beaver Lake; finishing spring 2027

    Removals are based on public-safety risk assessment. Work runs October to April, outside bird nesting season. No pesticides are used. [FM] [J25]

    What happens to the wood

    Felled trees are kept as wildlife trees, nurse logs and woody debris. More than 2,000 t of brush and fine fuels have been cleared to reduce wildfire risk. Fir and cedar went to the Musqueam, Squamish and Tsleil-Waututh Nations for cultural use. Other logs went to a sort yard in Squamish and were sold. [FM] [J26]

    Replanting for a more diverse forest

    About 25,000 seedlings were planted in spring 2024, 29,000+ in spring 2025 and 22,000+ in spring 2026. The species are western red cedar, Douglas fir, Sitka spruce, grand fir, red alder and Pacific yew. The aim is to reduce the over-representation of hemlock. The seedlings come from a three-year donation by Western Forest Products. [FM]

    Forest history

    Many nurse stumps remain from logging in the 1860s. Major windstorms hit in 1934, 1962 (Typhoon Freda) and 2006, when about 10,000 trees fell. Fire has been suppressed since 1888. The Hollow Tree is the stump of a 700–800-year-old western red cedar. A small remnant old-growth stand survives next to Tunnel Trail and Pipeline Road. [BL] [SOPEI] [LM] [FMP]

    How long until old growth? Speculative

    In BC, coastal old growth legally means older than 250 years. [BC gov] In a Vancouver Island study, second-growth stands reached old-growth structure in as little as 112 years, but most took 200 to more than 250 years. [CJFR 2011] Thinning with "skips and gaps" sped up some structural features. [USFS] No source puts a number on how many years active management could save in Stanley Park.

    Wildlife: a city that shares its edges

    The waterfront that was kept free of freeways also turned out to be a gift to wildlife.

    Great blue herons

    The colony at 2099 Beach Ave has been there since 2001, and herons have nested in the park since at least 1921. It is one of the largest urban colonies in North America. [WL] In 2025, monitors counted about 100 nests. [SPES 2025]

    Beaver Lake

    Beavers came back in 2008 after more than 60 years away. The lake is filling in quickly because of water lilies planted in 1937 and sediment. River otters also live at the lake. [WL] [BLE] [SPBL]

    Beaver Creek salmon

    Beaver Creek is one of three remaining salmon-bearing streams in Vancouver, with coho and cutthroat trout. The coho are hatchery fry released since 1990. There is no natural salmon run into Beaver Lake or Lost Lagoon. [BL] [BC]

    Lost Lagoon

    This former tidal arm of Coal Harbour is now a freshwater bird sanctuary, 16 ha in area and on average 1.2 m deep. Sources disagree on when the causeway closed it off: 1916 or the 1920s. [LL] [EAP]

    Mammals

    Coyotes, raccoons, Douglas and northern flying squirrels, invasive eastern gray squirrels, mink, bats and beavers live in the park. Harbour seals are often seen from the seawall. Deer show up only occasionally. [WL] [SPW]

    Birds & amphibians

    Bald eagles nest in the park. You can also find pelagic cormorants on the cliffs, owls, barn and tree swallows, and Ensatina salamanders. [SPW] [FMP]

    Speculative If the city treats its seawall, streams and the mixed-species forest of Stanley Park as one connected habitat network, Vancouver could become one of the few big North American cities where wildlife has more room in 2075 than it had in 1975.

    Why it matters: None of this keeps itself going. What you can do →

    6. What you can do

    The 1960s residents used ordinary tools. Those tools still work, whoever you vote for.

    1. Vote on October 17. Ask candidates of any affiliation how they would handle the viaduct lands, transit, the shoreline and housing.
    2. Read the plan before the hearing. The Northeast False Creek Plan, the Broadway Plan, the False Creek Coastal Adaptation Plan and the Park Board's Stanley Park forest work are all public. The 1967 hearings worked because people asked whether alternatives had been studied.
    3. Show up. Public hearings, open houses and consultations are where the 1967 route was undone.
    4. Talk to your neighbours. SPOTA worked because block captains carried news house to house, across language and background.
    5. Look beyond the city line. Much of the region's growth and traffic lands in Burnaby, Surrey and the suburbs. A fair future has to account for them.

    “The city avoided an old mistake. Whether it makes the most of that will depend on the same habits of attention and participation.”
